### Verdict

**I would hand the diplomat the Azure (C) transcript — narrowly, and only with a numeric
normalisation pass in front of it.**

The two arms are close on raw error count, and Azure's errors are more numerous in the
"looks broken" category: "resolution 27/34", "two thousand and twelve sixty seven", "twenty
two fifty five", two fifty-word stretches with no capitals or full stops, "the front
welcomes". Read cold, Azure looks like the sloppier transcript. But almost all of that is
a *number-and-punctuation formatter* failure, not a listening failure — Azure heard the
digits and wrote them badly, and a deterministic post-processor over a closed vocabulary
of UN resolution numbers would repair most of it. What Azure does not do, anywhere in 2,192
words, is invent content or misattribute speech. Its diarization is clean: seven stable
labels, the President consistently spk 1, no leakage across turn boundaries.

AssemblyAI is the better-formatted transcript and the more pleasant read, and on the
United States and Sierra Leone statements — native English, no interpretation — it is
excellent. But it fails in the one way that cannot be caught downstream. Row 113 has
Algeria stating that its vote "upholds the core objectives and legitimacy of Daesh and
al-Qaeda"; row 114 adds an entire sentence Algeria never said; row 75 has Russia objecting
to "the question of SDG"; row 73 puts the fragment "Let me propose" inside a Russian
national position. All four are fluent, grammatical, quotable English. No spell-check, no
reviewer skimming for garble, and no reader will catch them — that is exactly what makes
them dangerous in a document whose purpose is to record what a State said. Add four
speaker-label leaks and a fabricated "Aye" during a show-of-hands vote, and the failure
profile is confabulation rather than mishearing.

The asymmetry that decides it: Azure's worst failures are *visible and mechanical*;
AssemblyAI's worst failures are *invisible and semantic*. For a procurement decision on
Council records, take the transcript whose errors announce themselves.

Two caveats on that verdict. First, it is one meeting — 19.6 minutes, six statements,
three of them interpreted. Second, Azure's C2 (delisting → "the listing") is an
invisible-semantic error of exactly the kind I have just penalised AssemblyAI for; the
difference is that Azure produced one and AssemblyAI produced four. If a second meeting
showed Azure at three or four such errors, the verdict would flip.
